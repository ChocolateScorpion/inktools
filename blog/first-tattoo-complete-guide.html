First tattoo: the complete guide

Getting your first tattoo is a bigger decision than most people realize — not because you're likely to regret it (most people don't, and most wish they'd started sooner), but because getting exactly what you want requires information that isn't obvious until you're already in the chair. From choosing a design that will mean something to you in twenty years, to finding an artist whose specific skills match your vision, to understanding what happens during and after the session: this guide covers all of it, in the order you'll need it.

Step 1 — Choosing your design

The most important thing to know about choosing a tattoo design is this: start with what matters to you, not with what's popular right now. Trends date quickly and look dated even faster. A design that carries genuine personal meaning — whether that's an image, a symbol, a piece of text, or an abstract form that resonates with you — has a quality of permanence that a trend-driven choice rarely achieves.

Common categories to think through:

  • Meaningful symbols: Religious or spiritual symbols, cultural symbols, personal emblems — things that represent a framework you actually live by.
  • Portraits: A person or animal that matters deeply to you. These work best as black and grey realism and require a specialist artist.
  • Abstract or geometric: Designs where the aesthetic itself is the point — mandala work, sacred geometry, abstract patterns.
  • Nature: Animals, botanicals, landscapes. These translate well across virtually every tattoo style and have the advantage of being timeless.
  • Text and quotes: Meaningful words, dates, names. Choose font and placement carefully — text tattoos live and die by their execution.
  • Pop culture: The riskiest category for longevity. Something from a show or film you love now may feel dated in a decade. If you choose this, choose it knowing you're capturing a specific time in your life rather than betting on the design aging well.

The most useful rule: sit with the design for at least 2–4 weeks before booking. Look at it daily. If you're still certain at the end of that period, book. If doubts surface, wait longer. The tattoo will still be there when you're ready.

Research tip: Save images that appeal to you across multiple sources — Instagram, Pinterest, tattoo convention photos, art books. Look at what you've collected after two weeks. The themes and aesthetics that keep appearing are likely what you actually want.

Step 2 — Finding the right artist

This step is where most first-timers underinvest, and it's one of the biggest predictors of satisfaction with the final result. The critical thing to understand: style match is everything. Every tattoo artist has a style — or a small set of related styles — that they execute well. Asking a Traditional artist to do photorealism, or asking a fine line specialist to execute Japanese tattooing, is asking them to work outside their actual expertise. The result is rarely as good as either would be in their strength.

How to find the right artist

Instagram is currently the most useful tool for finding tattoo artists by style. Search the style name as a hashtag (e.g., #finelinetattoo, #geometrictattoo, #blackandgreytattoo) and look at whose work consistently resonates with you. Then look at their full portfolio — not just the best pieces, but the range of work. Do they consistently execute at that quality, or is the feed cherry-picked?

Google reviews tell you about the experience side: professionalism, communication, cleanliness, and how the artist handles concerns. Negative reviews about the artistic quality of the work are more significant than complaints about wait times or scheduling.

Tattoo conventions bring together artists from across the country and internationally, often with a focus on specific styles. Walking a convention gives you a chance to see healed work in person, talk to artists directly, and compare skill levels across many practitioners at once.

Word of mouth from people who have tattoos in the style you want is still one of the most reliable routes. If you see a tattoo you love on a stranger, it's completely normal and acceptable in tattoo culture to ask who their artist was.

Red flags to watch for

  • No portfolio, or a very thin portfolio — experience matters in tattooing
  • Unwillingness or inability to show healed photos of their work
  • Defensiveness when asked questions or pushback about wanting to see examples
  • A shop that doesn't appear clean and well-maintained
  • Pressure to book immediately or make quick decisions
  • No consultation offered before the session
  • Prices that seem extremely low — quality tattooing is skilled labor and priced accordingly

Step 3 — The consultation

A consultation is a meeting with your artist before your appointment to discuss the design, size, placement, and logistics. Most reputable artists offer consultations for new clients, and you should request one if it's not automatically offered. A good consultation is where the design becomes real.

What to bring to your consultation:

  • Reference images — photos, printouts, AI-generated concepts, sketches — showing the subject, style, and mood you're going for. The more specific your references, the more useful the conversation.
  • A sense of the size you're thinking about. Not a fixed answer, but a starting point — roughly palm-sized, forearm-length, etc.
  • Your preferred placement and whether you have any flexibility on it.
  • Any design elements that are non-negotiable versus flexible.

What to ask your artist:

  • What's the estimated price range for the piece?
  • How long will the session likely take?
  • What healing should I expect, and what's the touch-up policy?
  • Will the artist design the piece, or am I bringing a completed design?
  • Can I see a sketch or rough before the appointment?

Use the consultation to assess the artist's communication style as much as their design input. You're going to spend hours in close proximity with this person — a good working relationship matters.

Step 4 — Booking and the deposit

Once you've decided on an artist and had a consultation, you'll typically pay a deposit to secure your appointment. A standard deposit runs between $50 and $200 depending on the artist and region, and is deducted from the final price of the tattoo. Deposits are normal and legitimate practice — they protect the artist's time against cancellations.

Understand the rescheduling policy before you book. Most artists allow one reschedule with advance notice (often 48–72 hours minimum) without losing your deposit. Cancellations with insufficient notice typically forfeit the deposit. This is fair — a last-minute cancellation leaves the artist with an empty session and no income.

Good artists often book out weeks or months in advance, particularly for larger or more complex pieces. If an artist you want has a three-month wait, that wait is usually a sign of quality, not a reason to look elsewhere. Book early and use the waiting period to continue refining your reference images and design ideas.

Step 5 — Preparation in the days before

How you prepare in the 48 hours before your appointment has a real effect on how the session goes and how well you heal. Here's what to do — and what to avoid:

What to do before your session

  • Stay well hydrated in the 48 hours before — well-hydrated skin takes ink more evenly and heals better
  • Get a solid night of sleep before the appointment day
  • Eat a full, balanced meal 1–2 hours before your session begins — blood sugar maintenance helps you manage pain and prevents lightheadedness
  • Wear comfortable, loose clothing that gives your artist easy access to the placement area — you don't want to be awkwardly half-undressed during the session
  • Shower and clean the skin thoroughly on the morning of your appointment
  • Bring snacks and water for longer sessions
  • Bring something to occupy your attention — headphones, a podcast, a book

What to avoid before your session

  • Alcohol in the 24 hours before — it thins blood, causing more bleeding during the session and potentially affecting ink saturation
  • Aspirin or ibuprofen on the day of — these also thin blood. Paracetamol/acetaminophen is okay if you need a pain reliever
  • Excessive caffeine — it can increase sensitivity and cause jitteriness that makes sitting still harder
  • Lotion or moisturizer on the area being tattooed on the day of — clean, dry skin is easiest for the artist to work with
  • Sunburn on the area — tattooing over sunburned skin is not possible and will need to be rescheduled
  • Skin irritants or new skincare products in the week before — anything that causes redness or sensitivity on the area

Step 6 — What to expect during the session

Knowing what's going to happen takes a lot of the anxiety out of the first experience. Here's a typical sequence for a medium-sized piece:

  1. Stencil application. Your artist will apply a transfer stencil of the design to your skin, positioning it in the agreed placement. This is your moment to evaluate the exact size and position before any ink goes in. Don't be afraid to ask for repositioning — a slight adjustment now beats living with a misaligned placement forever.
  2. Design approval. The artist will show you the stencil placement in a mirror and ask for your approval. Look carefully. Check the orientation, the size relative to the area, and whether it's centered or positioned the way you discussed. Speak up if anything isn't right.
  3. Setup. The artist prepares their inks, needles, and equipment. This is the last moment before the session proper begins — a good time to use the bathroom and settle in.
  4. Outlining. The outline is typically the most intense part of the session. The sensation is a sharp, scratching feeling — often described as a cat scratch or a hot scratch. The first line is usually the most surprising, because nothing prepares you for the exact sensation until you experience it. It becomes familiar quickly.
  5. Shading and color fill. After the outline, shading and coloring often feel different — a broader, buzz-like pressure rather than the sharper scratch of the outline needle. Many people find this part more tolerable than the outline, though it varies by style.
  6. Breaks. For sessions over an hour, breaks are normal and good artists will offer them or encourage you to ask for one. Use them to eat a snack, drink water, and move around. Brief breaks don't significantly extend the session and substantially improve your endurance.
  7. Wrap-up. The artist cleans the area, photographs the finished piece, and applies the initial wrap or bandage. They'll give you aftercare instructions — listen carefully and ask questions about anything unclear.

The most important thing during the session: Sit still. Movement when the artist is working on fine detail can cause lines to deviate. If you need to move, ask for a brief break. Good artists appreciate this far more than clients who try to tough through discomfort while subtly shifting.

Step 7 — Managing pain during the session

Tattoo pain is real but manageable. Most people describe it as more tolerable than they anticipated — partly because the experience is so novel, and partly because the body's natural pain responses kick in and the sensation becomes background noise as you adapt to it. A few techniques help:

Breathing is the most accessible tool. Long, slow exhales during the passes that hurt most reduce the sensation noticeably. It's the same principle used in any pain management context — controlled breathing activates the parasympathetic nervous system and takes the edge off acute pain.

Distraction is highly effective. Headphones with music or a podcast you're genuinely engaged in occupy the attention that would otherwise focus on the pain. Many experienced collectors describe barely noticing the pain when they're deep in an audiobook during a session.

Numbing creams (like EMLA or similar topical anesthetics) are available and can reduce surface sensation significantly. However, discuss their use with your artist first — some artists find that numbing creams affect the texture of the skin in ways that alter how ink behaves, and not all artists are comfortable working on numbed skin. If you want to use one, bring it up at the consultation.

Eating and hydrating before and during the session is genuinely effective pain management. Low blood sugar dramatically lowers pain tolerance and increases the likelihood of feeling faint or nauseated. A substantial meal before the session and snacks during a long one make a measurable difference.

Step 8 — Aftercare: the critical first two weeks

The session produces the design; aftercare determines how it heals. A tattoo that's executed perfectly can heal poorly with bad aftercare, and good aftercare can rescue a slightly troubled start. The first two weeks are when the skin is actively healing and the tattoo is most vulnerable.

The first 24 hours

Your artist will wrap the tattoo before you leave — either with traditional cling film or with a second-skin breathable bandage (like Saniderm or Tegaderm). If it's traditional cling film, remove it after 2–4 hours, wash the tattoo gently, and leave it unwrapped to breathe. If it's a breathable bandage, it can typically stay on for 24–72 hours — follow your artist's specific instructions, as these vary by product and application.

Washing and moisturizing

Wash the tattoo gently with lukewarm water and unscented, mild soap — nothing antibacterial, nothing fragrant. Fragrance and antibacterial agents can cause reactions on healing skin. Lather in your palm, gently apply, rinse thoroughly, and pat dry with a clean paper towel (not a fabric towel, which can harbor bacteria and snag on healing skin).

Apply a thin layer of unscented lotion — Lubriderm, Aveeno, or CeraVe work well. Thin is the operative word: a thick coating smothers the skin and creates a moist environment that can breed bacteria. The lotion should be barely perceptible on the skin, not a visible coat. Apply 2–3 times a day as needed to prevent the tattoo from drying out and cracking.

Avoid: Vaseline (too occlusive), heavily scented lotions, products containing alcohol, and anything you wouldn't put on a wound. Also avoid Aquaphor in the early days — while popular in the tattoo community, it can be too heavy for some skin types and some artists prefer lighter alternatives.

What to avoid during healing

  • Direct sun on the tattoo — cover it with clothing when outdoors during the healing period
  • Submerging in water — pools, oceans, hot tubs, and baths. Showers are fine; soaking is not.
  • Picking or scratching — the tattoo will peel and itch as it heals. This is normal. Let the flaking happen naturally.
  • Tight clothing rubbing against the area during the healing period
  • Working out intensely in ways that stretch or sweat heavily over the tattooed area in the first week

The peeling phase

Around days 4–7, the tattoo will begin to peel — thin flakes of skin, often with ink visible in the flakes. This is entirely normal. The ink that flakes off is only surface ink; the ink that matters is locked deeper in the dermis and stays behind. Do not pick the flakes. Let them fall naturally. Picking can pull ink that's still anchoring and create spots or gaps in the healed tattoo.

Step 9 — Long-term care

Once the tattoo is fully healed — typically 3–4 weeks for surface healing, and 3–6 months for the skin to fully settle — the maintenance requirements shift from intensive to minimal but consistent:

  • SPF 30 or higher on healed tattoos whenever they're exposed to sun. UV radiation is the primary driver of long-term tattoo fading. This single habit makes more difference to how a tattoo looks in ten years than almost anything else.
  • Regular moisturizing. Healthy, hydrated skin keeps ink looking more vibrant than dry, cracked skin.
  • Touch-ups. Once the tattoo is fully settled (3–6 months), evaluate whether any areas healed patchily or need refreshing. Most reputable artists offer free or reduced-price touch-ups for their own work within a specified window.

Frequently asked questions

How much does a first tattoo cost?
Pricing varies enormously by artist, location, and piece size. Small pieces (playing card size or smaller) from a reputable artist typically run $100–$250 in most US cities. Medium pieces (palm-sized to hand-sized) are typically $200–$500+. Large pieces are most often quoted as an hourly rate, which ranges from $100–$300 per hour depending on the artist's experience and location. Shops also typically have a minimum charge (usually $80–$150) regardless of piece size. Price shopping tattoos is generally a bad strategy — the more you're fixated on finding the cheapest option, the more likely you are to end up with a tattoo you don't love.
How much does a tattoo hurt on a scale of 1–10?
For most first-timers on a low-pain placement (outer arm, thigh, shoulder), the honest answer is around 3–4 out of 10. It's a noticeable, sustained sensation — not something you'd describe as comfortable — but well within what most people can handle without significant difficulty. High-pain placements (ribs, sternum, spine, hands) can reach 7–8 out of 10 for some people. Pain is also highly individual: hunger, anxiety, fatigue, and general pain tolerance all affect the experience. The sensation is also novel in a way that makes it hard to rate until you've experienced it — most people's honest post-tattoo assessment is "not as bad as I expected."
Can I bring a friend to my tattoo session?
Usually yes, though shop policies vary and it's worth confirming when you book. Having a trusted friend present can help with nerves, and for longer sessions, company is genuinely useful. A few things to keep in mind: your friend should be calm and unobtrusive — an anxious support person can increase your own anxiety rather than reduce it. They also shouldn't try to engage the artist in extended conversation during detailed work, as focus matters. In smaller shops or busy studios, the artist may prefer you come alone. Just ask in advance.
What if I don't like the stencil?
Say so, clearly and immediately. The stencil stage is explicitly your opportunity to evaluate the design in situ before any ink goes in. Artists expect clients to request adjustments — to the position, size, or alignment. This is not an inconvenience; it's part of the process. What you should not do is sit with doubts and let the session proceed hoping it will look okay. No professional tattoo artist would rather you say nothing and regret the result than ask for an adjustment now. If the stencil is significantly different from what you discussed and the artist can't address your concerns, it is acceptable to stop before the session begins.
How long does healing take?
Surface healing — the top layer of skin closing over — typically takes 2–3 weeks. The peeling and flaking phase happens in this window. The tattoo will look hazy or milky during this period as new skin grows over the ink — this is temporary and resolves as the surface skin clarifies. Full healing, meaning the skin has settled completely and the ink has locked into the dermis, takes 3–6 months. You should wait for full healing before assessing whether a touch-up is needed, and before evaluating the final color and line clarity of the piece.
Can I exercise after getting a tattoo?
Light activity is generally fine from day 2 or 3 onward, but intense exercise deserves some care during the first two weeks. Sweating heavily over a healing tattoo creates a moist environment that can affect healing and increase infection risk. High-impact movement that stretches the tattooed area — like weightlifting with an arm tattoo, or running with a leg tattoo — creates friction and movement that can affect how the tattoo settles. If you're a regular exerciser, keep the tattooed area clean after workouts and avoid submerging in chlorinated pools, which can draw ink and cause irritation in healing skin.
What's the best placement for a first tattoo?
The outer upper arm is the most recommended first-tattoo placement — low pain, reliable healing, large enough canvas for a meaningful design, and easy to cover for professional contexts. The outer forearm is another strong option, slightly more visible and with similarly easy healing. Both placements let you see and appreciate your tattoo easily during the healing process, which matters more than you might expect. For your first tattoo, avoid ribs, hands, feet, sternum, and neck — not because these are bad placements, but because starting with a lower-difficulty placement lets you assess your response to tattooing before committing to a more challenging one.
