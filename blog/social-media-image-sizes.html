Social media image sizes: the complete guide for 2026

Uploading an image at the wrong dimensions means the platform will crop it, stretch it, or add awkward padding to fill the frame. Worse, every social media platform aggressively recompresses images on upload — and if your image is already compressed at the wrong size, you end up with a double-compressed, blurry result. Getting the dimensions right before you upload eliminates both problems entirely. Here are the verified image specifications for every major platform in 2026.

Why image dimensions matter on social media

Every major social media platform recompresses your images when you upload them. There's no avoiding this — it's how platforms manage the enormous storage and bandwidth costs of serving billions of images. The practical consequence is that the image your followers see is not the file you uploaded; it's a re-encoded version that has already lost some quality.

When you upload an image at the correct dimensions, the platform recompresses from a full-resolution source. When you upload at incorrect dimensions — too large, too small, or the wrong aspect ratio — the platform first crops or scales the image and then recompresses. That double processing compounds quality loss and can introduce artefacts that weren't in your original image.

Getting dimensions right also matters for:

  • Ad campaigns: Most platforms reject image ads that don't meet their exact size and aspect ratio requirements. A wrong-sized image can cause your ad to be disapproved outright, wasting creative work and budget.
  • Engagement: Images that appear cropped in unexpected ways, or with blurry detail from double compression, receive measurably fewer interactions. The visual first impression matters enormously in feeds where users scroll past dozens of posts per minute.
  • Profile presentation: Profile pictures and cover photos displayed at incorrect dimensions create an immediate signal of low professionalism — something that matters particularly for business accounts and personal brands.

Instagram displays profile pictures as circles, so anything important in your profile photo should be centred with safe margins on all sides. For Stories and Reels, keep text and important visual elements within the safe zone — approximately the middle 80% of the 1080×1920 frame — as the top and bottom edges may be covered by UI elements like the username bar and action buttons.

Instagram prefers JPG for photographs and PNG for graphics. Maximum file size is 30 MB per image, though you should be well below that with properly optimised images. Video thumbnails for Reels follow the same 9:16 ratio.

Instagram tip: The 4:5 portrait ratio (1080×1350 px) takes up significantly more vertical space in the feed than a square post — roughly 25% more screen real estate. For brands focused on feed visibility, portrait posts consistently outperform square and landscape in engagement studies.

Design your cover so that the most important content sits in a central band roughly 640 pixels wide and 312 pixels tall — that zone is safe on both screen types.

Facebook compresses images relatively aggressively. For feed posts, uploading at exactly 1200×630 px in sRGB colour space with a quality-80 JPG or a PNG will yield the best results after Facebook's recompression.

Twitter's feed crops single images to approximately a 2:1 ratio for the in-feed preview. If your image is taller than that — for example, a portrait photograph — only the top portion will be visible until the user taps to expand. Twitter does attempt to detect faces and focus points and adjusts its crop accordingly, but you shouldn't rely on this for important composed images. When in doubt, a 16:9 landscape image is the safest choice for feed visibility.

For multiple images in a single tweet, Twitter tiles them differently depending on whether you upload 2, 3, or 4 images. For multi-image posts, square crops (1:1) tend to survive Twitter's tiling layout most gracefully.

The YouTube channel banner deserves special attention because it displays very differently across devices. On a TV screen, the entire 2560×1440 image is visible. YouTube defines a "safe area" of 1546×423 px centred in the banner — keep all text and important visual elements within that rectangle to ensure they're visible on every device.

For video thumbnails, high contrast and readable text at small sizes are more important than pixel dimensions. Your thumbnail will often be displayed at 168×94 px in search results — design for legibility at that size first.

Tips for batch resizing across platforms

Resizing a single image for seven platforms would be exhausting if you did each one manually. A more efficient workflow:

  • Start with the largest format you need. For most creators, that's YouTube channel art at 2560×1440 px or an Instagram Story at 1080×1920 px. Design at that size first.
  • Crop for each platform, don't resize-then-crop. Maintaining the original large file and cropping fresh for each platform preserves maximum quality at each output size.
  • Use templates. Set up a master design file with guides showing the safe zones for each platform. Many design tools let you export multiple artboards at once.
  • Compress after cropping. Run the final cropped image through a compressor before uploading. This gives the platform the best possible source to work from and avoids the double-compression penalty.

File format tips per platform

Beyond dimensions, the file format you upload affects quality after the platform's recompression:

  • JPG: Best for photographs and complex images. Use for Instagram posts, Facebook posts, LinkedIn posts, and YouTube thumbnails. Upload at 85–95% quality to give the platform a good source to compress from.
  • PNG: Best for graphics, logos, screenshots, and images containing text. PNG's lossless encoding means the platform starts from a pixel-perfect source. Use for profile pictures and any image where text legibility is critical.
  • WebP: Some platforms now accept WebP and will serve it when the browser supports it. When in doubt, JPG or PNG are safer if you're uncertain about platform support.

Frequently asked questions

What image size is best for Instagram in 2026?
For standard feed posts, 1080×1080 px (1:1 square) is the safest choice because it looks consistent across all devices and avoids any cropping. If you want to maximise feed visibility and don't mind a portrait layout, 1080×1350 px (4:5 ratio) takes up more vertical space in the feed and tends to outperform square posts in engagement. For Stories and Reels, use 1080×1920 px (9:16 vertical). Always upload in JPG for photos and PNG for graphics, under 30 MB.
Does Facebook compress my images?
Yes, significantly. Facebook recompresses all uploaded images using its own JPEG encoder, which applies moderate to aggressive compression to manage storage and bandwidth costs. The best way to minimise quality loss is to upload at the correct dimensions (1200×630 px for feed posts), use sRGB colour space, and save your source image at 80–90% JPEG quality. Uploading a PNG can sometimes produce better results for images containing text or sharp geometric shapes, as Facebook will then compress from a lossless source rather than double-compressing an already-lossy JPG.
What format should I use for social media images?
JPG for photographs and complex images; PNG for graphics, logos, screenshots, and images with text. JPG at 85–95% quality is smaller and works well for platform uploads. PNG ensures the platform starts from a perfect source for text-heavy graphics. Avoid GIF for static images — it limits you to 256 colours. WebP is increasingly accepted but JPG and PNG are universally safe choices across all platforms and contexts including email, direct sharing, and third-party tools.
Can I use the same image on all social media platforms?
You can, but you'll get suboptimal results on most of them. Different platforms have fundamentally different aspect ratios — Instagram Stories are 9:16 vertical, YouTube thumbnails are 16:9 horizontal, Pinterest pins are 2:3 portrait, and Twitter in-feed images are best at 16:9. Uploading a square image to all of them means each platform will crop or pad it differently. The most practical approach is to design once at the largest needed size, then export cropped versions at each platform's ideal dimensions before uploading.
How do I resize an image without losing quality?
Always resize down, never up. Scaling a small image to a larger size (upscaling) creates blurriness because the software has to invent pixels that weren't in the original. Scaling a large image down to a smaller size always produces better results than scaling a small image up to the needed dimensions. Use high-quality resampling algorithms (Lanczos or bicubic) rather than fast nearest-neighbour resampling, which can produce jaggy edges. What is the maximum file size for Instagram?
Instagram's maximum file size for photos is 30 MB. However, uploading large files doesn't result in better quality after Instagram's recompression — it just increases your upload time. Instagram will compress your image regardless of the source file size. The practical sweet spot is to upload a well-optimised JPG or PNG at the correct dimensions (1080 px wide) at 80–90% quality, which will typically produce a file in the 200–500 KB range — well within limits and fast to upload. Instagram video file sizes have different limits depending on format.
