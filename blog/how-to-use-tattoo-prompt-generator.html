How to use the Tattoo Prompt Generator

The Tattoo Prompt Generator turns a few selections into a detailed, ready-to-use prompt for any AI image tool. Here's a walkthrough of each field, plus real prompt examples by tattoo style so you can see exactly what kind of output to expect.

Step by step

  1. Describe the subject. This is the core of your idea — what the tattoo is actually of. Be as specific as you can. "Wolf" is fine, but "wolf howling at a crescent moon, seen from below" gives the AI much more to work with and produces a more useful reference.
  2. Select a tattoo style. Choose the style that most closely matches what you're going for. This is the single most impactful setting — it changes everything from linework weight to color approach to overall aesthetic. If you're unsure, "Fine Line" and "Blackwork" tend to produce the cleanest, most universally readable references.
  3. Pick up to 4 moods. Moods layer adjectives on top of the style. "Dark + Mystical" produces very different results than "Elegant + Delicate" even with the same subject and style. Stick to moods that actually coexist — don't pick both "Fierce" and "Delicate" unless you're intentionally going for contrast.
  4. Add specific elements (optional). List objects, symbols, or motifs you want in the composition. Separated by commas: "roses, skull, chain, crescent moon." These get inserted directly into the prompt and guide the AI toward specific imagery.
  5. Choose placement. Selecting a placement adds compositional guidance — a forearm piece needs a different layout than a full back piece. If you haven't decided on placement yet, leave it as "Not specified."
  6. Set color preference and detail level. Black & grey is the default and produces the most stencil-like references. Crank up the detail level for complex, intricate concepts; keep it low for clean, minimal designs.
  7. Click Generate. You'll get two prompts — a Universal prompt that works in any AI tool, and a Midjourney prompt with platform-specific parameters already set.

Quick tip: Generate, paste into your AI tool, look at the result, then come back and adjust one thing. Change the mood, or add an element, or bump the detail level. Iterating in small steps gets you to a useful reference faster than trying to get the perfect prompt on the first try.

Where to paste your prompts

  • Midjourney — paste the Midjourney prompt directly into Discord using /imagine. The parameters (--ar, --stylize, --v) are already included.
  • ChatGPT / DALL-E — paste the Universal prompt as your message. You can add "Generate this as a tattoo reference image" at the start if needed.
  • Ideogram — paste the Universal prompt in the generation field. Works especially well for graphic and geometric styles.
  • Adobe Firefly — paste the Universal prompt. Useful when you need commercially safe reference imagery.

Remember: The generated image is a reference, not the final design. Show it to your artist as a direction — "something like this but in your style" — not as the artwork itself. The tattoo design is always the artist's work.

Understanding AI art parameters

When you use the Midjourney prompt output, you'll see parameters appended at the end — flags that control how the AI interprets and renders the image. Understanding what these do helps you get better results without guessing.

--ar (aspect ratio) controls the shape of the output image. --ar 1:1 produces a square, which works well for stencil-style references and chest or back placement concepts. --ar 2:3 gives a portrait orientation, ideal for arm and leg pieces where you want to see how the design fills a vertical space.

--v 7 specifies the Midjourney version. Version 7 is the current model and produces the most coherent tattoo-style imagery. Older versions behave differently and may give inconsistent results with tattoo prompts.

--style raw reduces Midjourney's built-in "beautification" and produces a more literal interpretation of your prompt. For tattoo references, this is usually preferable — you want the AI to follow your specific brief rather than apply a general artistic veneer on top of it.

--stylize controls how creatively the AI interprets the prompt versus how literally it follows it. The range is 0–1000. At --stylize 0, the output follows the prompt very literally — what you write is what you get, with minimal creative embellishment. At --stylize 100, the AI adds more of its own interpretation while still respecting the general direction. Most useful tattoo references come from stylize values between 0 and 200.

Recommended defaults: Start with --ar 1:1 --v 7 --style raw --stylize 50 for most tattoo reference work. Lower --stylize toward 0 if you want the AI to stay closer to your exact prompt wording. Raise it toward 200 if the results feel too mechanical.

How to refine your prompt for better results

The first prompt you generate is rarely the final one you use. Getting useful tattoo references from AI is an iterative process — knowing what to adjust makes each pass faster and more productive.

Be specific about style. Don't just say "tattoo" — say "American traditional tattoo style" or "single needle fine line tattoo." The more precisely you name the style, the more accurately the AI matches it. Vague style descriptors produce generic output that isn't useful as a consultation reference.

Use real tattoo terminology. Words like "linework," "shading," "negative space," "stippling," "blackwork fill," and "flash design" are part of the AI's training data in a tattooing context. Using them pulls the output in the right direction in a way that generic art terms don't.

Describe the mood explicitly. "Dark and moody," "clean and minimal," "bold and graphic," "soft and delicate" — mood descriptors have a strong effect on composition, line weight, and overall feel. They often make more difference than adding more subject detail.

Include what you don't want. Exclusions actively steer the AI away from common defaults. "No text, no color, clean black lines only" is a simple exclusion clause that prevents a lot of unwanted output. "No watercolor effects, no color splashes, no gradient fills" is useful when you want pure linework and the AI keeps adding painterly effects.

Change one thing per iteration. If you change style, mood, elements, and detail level all at once, you won't know which change improved the result. Adjust one variable at a time and compare. This takes more iterations but converges on a strong reference much faster than wholesale prompt rewrites.

Using generated images with your tattoo artist

AI-generated images work best as a conversation starter, not a final brief. The way you present them to your artist affects how useful the consultation ends up being.

Print 2–3 variations and bring them to the consultation. Digital references on a phone screen are easy to scroll past. Printed references get laid out on the counter, pointed at, and talked about in detail. If you've generated multiple variations exploring different moods or compositions, printing the two or three strongest gives the artist concrete material to react to.

Highlight specific elements you like from each. Rather than pointing at one image and saying "I want this," go through each reference and mark what works. "I like the line weight in this one, the composition in that one, and the negative space usage in the third." This gives the artist genuine creative input rather than a design to replicate.

Let the artist know it's an AI reference. Most tattoo artists appreciate this transparency and many are now familiar with AI-generated imagery. Knowing it's AI helps them understand why certain elements may not be technically realistic — AI images sometimes suggest impossible linework or shading that wouldn't hold on skin — and lets them bring their expertise to those decisions.

The artist will redraw in their own style anyway. An AI reference is not a stencil-ready design. A good artist will take the concept, adapt it to work on skin with proper line weights and composition, and produce an original piece informed by — not copied from — the reference. AI references work especially well for conveying a general aesthetic, showing rough size and placement concepts, and explaining abstract ideas that are hard to find real photo references for.

What to say in the consultation: "I generated this as a direction reference — I like the overall feel and composition, but I know it'll need to be redrawn for tattooing. What's your take?" This opens a collaborative conversation rather than presenting a design to be copied.

Frequently asked questions

What AI image generators work best with these prompts?
Midjourney consistently produces the strongest tattoo references, especially for illustrative styles like blackwork and neo-traditional. For geometric and clean graphic work, Ideogram is excellent; for portrait realism, DALL-E via ChatGPT tends to give the most usable results.
Can I use the generated prompts for free commercial tattoo designs?
The prompts themselves are yours to use however you like. Whether the AI-generated images produced from those prompts are commercially usable depends on the terms of service of the specific AI platform you use — Midjourney and Adobe Firefly have different licensing rules. The prompts are just text; the images carry the platform's terms.
How do I make the prompt more specific to my style?
Add descriptive elements and adjust the mood settings — these have the biggest impact on output. If you have an artist whose work you admire, describe that aesthetic with adjectives rather than naming the artist directly. Terms like "crisp single-pass linework," "high-contrast negative space," or "flat graphic fills" often sharpen the output significantly.
Does the generator work for fine line tattoos?
Yes — select "Fine Line" as the style and keep the detail level at 2–3. The generated prompt will include technical language about ultra-thin linework and single-needle precision. Midjourney with --style raw tends to give the cleanest fine line references with this approach.
Can I ask my tattoo artist to use these prompts?
Absolutely — many clients bring the generated images (not just the prompts) to consultations as direction references. You can share either the prompt or the resulting image; most artists find the image more immediately useful since it gives them something concrete to react to and build from.
What's the difference between Midjourney and Stable Diffusion for tattoo art?
Midjourney produces more aesthetically polished and stylistically coherent results out of the box, making it the easier choice for consultation references. Stable Diffusion offers more fine-grained control through community models specifically trained on tattoo art, but requires more setup and technical knowledge to get consistently good results.
